Japanese particles are function words used to give context to other words. Their aim is to provide context to a sentence and make it clearer, but they don’t change like verbs or nouns that get conjugated or become plural.

Particles are written in hiragana though some also have kanji forms which is currently out of scope of my knowledge. They follow the same phonetic rules of their Hiragana counterparts, with the exception of は (pronounced wa), へ (pronounced e) and を (pronounced o).

In languages such as English, word order can denote very clearly what the context and meaning behind a sentence is. In Japanese, however, particles can play a major role in making the subject, action, and where the action is acting on clear. For example:
```
“I eat a fish.”
Watashi wa sakana o tabemasu.
わたし は さかな を たべます。
I (subject) fish (object) eat

“A fish eats me.”
Watashi o sakana wa tabemasu.
わたし を さかな は たべます。
I (object) fish (subject) eat
```
Swapping the order of the two particles は and を has completely changed the meaning of the sentence.

From Matsumoto-san:
```
**More examples of** **_ga_** **and** **_wa_**  
1. Confessing to breaking a glass  
Imagine that someone is trying to find out who broke a glass. If I confess, I would say:  

> **Watashi ga** koppu o warimashita.  
> **I** broke the glass.

The important question is **who** broke the glass. The particle _ga_ places the informational focus on _watashi_ and identifies me as the person responsible.  

> Who broke the glass?  
> → **I** did.

2. Talking about yesterday  
Imagine that someone asks:  

> Kinō wa nani o shimashita ka?  
> What did you do yesterday?

I might answer:  

> Kinō wa **bābekyū o shimashita**.  
> Yesterday, I **had a barbecue**.

In this conversation, _kinō_—“yesterday”—has already been established as the topic. It is given information, so it is marked with _wa_. The important new information is the predicate **bābekyū o shimashita**, which explains what I did.  

> As for yesterday, I **had a barbecue**.

The particle _wa_ does not directly emphasize the predicate. Instead, it marks _kinō_ as the topic, allowing the predicate that follows it to carry the main new information.
```
